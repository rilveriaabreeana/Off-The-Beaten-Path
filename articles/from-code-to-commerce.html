THE JOURNEY · ARTICLE 01

From Code to Commerce

Finding My Path Beyond Software Engineering

How my first co-op helped me discover that a computer science degree can lead to more than one kind of career.

Career exploration·6 min read

When I first started searching for my co-op, I honestly had no idea what I wanted to do. I knew I was studying computer science, and I had always assumed that meant I would eventually become a software engineer. But as I started looking through job postings, I realized there were so many different career paths I had never even considered.

I remember feeling overwhelmed by the whole process. It seemed like everyone around me knew exactly what field they wanted to work in, while I was still trying to figure out what interested me. On top of that, I was putting a lot of pressure on myself to find a “good” co-op. I felt like I needed a role that would set me up for the rest of my career, which only made the search more stressful.

Looking back, I realize my first co-op wasn't supposed to determine my entire future. It was an opportunity to explore my interests, build on the skills I had learned in school, and figure out what kind of work I actually enjoyed.

Why I Chose Computer Science

Growing up, I was always someone who enjoyed problem-solving. I loved math, building LEGO sets, and working on projects where I could see a finished product. At the same time, I was also a pretty creative kid. I loved making things, doing DIY projects, and finding ways to bring my ideas to life.

When I was introduced to coding in high school, I felt like it combined both interests. I liked that I could use logic and problem-solving to create something of my own.

I remember building a game for a project in AP Computer Science A and really enjoying the process. Looking back, it was definitely simple compared to some of the things I've built in college, but the satisfaction I got from knowing I had created something myself was unbeatable.

When I decided to major in computer science, I didn't necessarily have a specific career in mind. Software engineering was the path I was most familiar with, so I naturally assumed that was where I would end up.

It wasn't until I started exploring co-op opportunities that I realized how many other possibilities existed.

The Co-op Search That Changed My Perspective

As my first co-op search went on, I started applying to a pretty wide range of positions. Honestly, if a job posting had the word “analyst” in the title and I met some of the qualifications, there was a good chance I was applying.

At the time, I wasn't necessarily applying because I had a strong interest in data analytics or business operations. I was mostly stressed about finding a co-op and wanted to keep my options open.

Eventually, that search led me to Wayfair, where I worked in operations analytics.

Before starting, I had a limited understanding of what working in business operations actually looked like. I knew data science and analytics existed as career paths, but I hadn't really considered how the skills I was learning in my computer science classes could translate into those roles.

What started as an attempt to keep my options open ended up introducing me to a completely different side of computer science.

Learning to Solve Problems Outside the Classroom

Starting my first co-op was definitely overwhelming. I was introduced to a variety of responsibilities pretty quickly, from writing SQL queries to pull supplier data to managing requests related to supplier settings. I also investigated performance issues, tracked delivery metrics, and attended meetings with other teams to understand how different parts of the business operated.

There was a lot to learn, but one thing I appreciated most was how much ownership I was given. I wasn't just observing how the team worked. I was contributing to projects that affected everyday business operations.

One experience that stood out was working on a larger SQL query for a peak-season planning initiative. Although I had coding experience from my classes, I wasn't necessarily confident going into the project. I had to learn how to apply those skills to real business data, and unlike a typical school assignment, my work was actually going to be used in the team's planning process.

That added a different level of responsibility. It wasn't just about writing code that worked. I needed to understand what information the team was looking for, how to retrieve it, and why it mattered.

As I became more comfortable with SQL, I started recognizing similarities between what I was doing at work and what I had learned in school. I was still using logic and problem-solving, but in a completely different context.

I began to realize that my computer science skills weren't limited to software engineering. They could also help answer business questions and support real decisions.

When Data Becomes a Business Decision

One of the things that surprised me most about operations analytics was how much problem-solving went into everyday business decisions.

One experience that stood out involved working with a supplier relationship manager whose supplier was consistently performing below 80% on our on-time shipping metric. The supplier was confused about why so many orders were being marked as late, so I took a closer look at their performance and the settings in our system.

After digging into the data, we realized the cutoff time listed in our system was different from the cutoff time the supplier believed they were operating under. Essentially, the supplier was working with one set of expectations while our system measured their performance against another.

Once we identified the issue, we got on a call with the supplier to explain what was happening and clarify the settings. After the discrepancy was resolved, we continued monitoring their performance over the following weeks.

Their on-time rate eventually climbed to over 92%, which was considered an excellent result for our suppliers.

What I found most interesting was that the solution wasn't necessarily complicated. The challenge was figuring out what was causing the problem in the first place. Without investigating the data and understanding how the supplier's settings affected their performance, the issue could have continued.

Experiences like this helped me appreciate the business side of analytics. I wasn't just working with numbers or writing queries. I was using data to investigate problems, communicate findings, and help make decisions that could improve performance.

Finding Direction Without Finding a Final Destination

By the end of my first co-op, I felt like I had a much better understanding of the direction I wanted to pursue. I enjoyed the fast-paced environment, investigating problems, and using data to come to conclusions.

At the same time, I still wasn't completely sure if supply chain operations was the field I wanted to work in long-term. I knew I enjoyed the work itself, but I wanted to explore what being an analyst in another industry might look like.

That experience changed how I approached my second co-op search.

Instead of applying to almost anything that sounded interesting, I was specifically looking for roles in business analytics. I wanted to continue working with data, take ownership of projects, and understand how businesses make decisions.

This eventually led me to my next co-op at New Balance, where I had the opportunity to explore business and technology in a different industry.

My first co-op didn't give me a final destination, but it gave me a clearer understanding of what I wanted to explore next.

It's Okay to Figure It Out Along the Way

If I could give myself advice at the beginning of my first co-op search, I would tell myself to be more open-minded and stop putting so much pressure on having everything figured out.

I was worried about not finding a co-op, ending up in a role I didn't enjoy, and falling behind everyone around me. Looking back, I was expecting myself to have answers to questions I hadn't even had the opportunity to explore yet.

My experience taught me that you don't always know what you enjoy until you actually try something new.

I think a lot of college students experience this uncertainty, even if it doesn't always seem that way. It's easy to compare yourself to other people and feel like you're behind because you haven't figured out exactly what you want to do.

There will be applications that go unanswered, interviews that don't turn into offers, and opportunities that don't work out the way you hoped. It's okay to feel discouraged. Those experiences are part of the process, and sometimes they help you understand what you're looking for.

I've always believed that everything happens for a reason and that things have a way of working out in the end. Learning to apply that mindset to my career has helped me become more comfortable with uncertainty.

The only timeline you need to follow is the one you set for yourself.

Give yourself the opportunity to explore different fields, take chances on roles that interest you, and learn from experiences that don't go exactly as planned.

For me, going from code to commerce wasn't about leaving computer science behind. It was about realizing that the skills I had been building could take me somewhere I hadn't originally expected.

And while I'm still figuring out exactly where I want to end up, I've learned that taking a path I didn't initially plan for doesn't mean I'm heading in the wrong direction.
