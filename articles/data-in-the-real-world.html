FIELD NOTE 02 · THE FIRST DETOUR

When Data Meets the Real World

What my co-ops taught me about the messy, collaborative, and sometimes unexpected reality of working in business analytics.

By Abreeana Rilveria

Before starting my first co-op, I honestly had no idea what working as a business or data analyst actually looked like. When I pictured someone working in analytics, I imagined them sitting at a desk, spending most of their day in Excel, looking at numbers, and creating dashboards to help businesses make decisions.

While those things are definitely part of the job, I quickly learned that analytics involves much more than I originally expected.

Through my experiences at Wayfair and New Balance, I've started to understand just how many different areas of a business analytics can touch. The tools and technical skills might overlap, but the problems you're solving can look completely different depending on the industry and team.

Perhaps even more surprising was discovering that working with data isn't always as straightforward as it sounds.

The Reality Behind the Numbers

I think it's easy to assume that large, well-established companies have their processes completely figured out. Before entering a professional environment, I imagined most businesses would have organized data, refined systems, and efficient workflows already in place.

But one of the first things I learned was that this isn't necessarily the case.

In both of my co-op experiences, I've encountered processes that were more complicated than they needed to be, data that wasn't always as organized as I expected, and workflows that had plenty of room for improvement.

At first, that surprised me. But as I became more familiar with my responsibilities, I realized that identifying these inefficiencies and figuring out how to improve them was actually a major part of the work.

Sometimes the challenge wasn't analyzing the data itself. It was understanding why a process worked the way it did, identifying where things were slowing down, and finding ways to make the work easier for everyone involved.

Another thing that surprised me was just how collaborative the work was.

At Wayfair, I felt like I was communicating with people throughout almost my entire eight-hour workday. Whether it was attending meetings, talking with coworkers in person, or sending messages through Slack, there was almost always some form of communication involved.

I had pictured analytics as a role where I'd spend most of my time working independently on my laptop. Instead, I was constantly collaborating with people to understand problems, clarify information, and figure out the best way to move forward.

It made me realize that being a good analyst isn't just about knowing how to work with data. It's also about understanding the people and processes behind the numbers.

When the Numbers Don't Tell the Whole Story

One of the biggest lessons I learned at Wayfair was that data doesn't always tell the full story. Numbers can identify patterns and potential problems, but understanding what's causing those problems often requires looking beyond a spreadsheet.

This became especially clear when my team was preparing for peak sales periods.

During these busier times, a lot of planning went into making sure suppliers could handle increased orders while still meeting shipping expectations. Historical performance data helped us understand how suppliers had performed previously and what we could realistically expect from them.

My responsibility was to communicate with suppliers and make sure they were prepared to meet those expectations. This involved confirming their operational capabilities and adjusting settings in our system.

Depending on the supplier, that could mean adding time to shipping estimates, adjusting pickup schedules, or setting limits on the number of orders they could receive in a day.

One situation that stood out involved a supplier whose performance had been particularly inconsistent. Some weeks, their shipping metrics looked great. Other weeks, their performance dropped significantly.

As we were deciding which suppliers to include in an initiative focused on faster shipping, we were hesitant about including them. We wanted participating suppliers to meet a higher standard, and their historical performance didn't consistently show that they could.

If we had made the decision based solely on the data, we probably would have excluded them.

Instead, we reached out to the supplier and their supplier relationship manager to better understand what was happening.

Through those conversations and a deeper investigation, we discovered that the issue wasn't necessarily the supplier's ability to fulfill orders quickly. There was a coordination issue between the supplier and the carrier responsible for pickups and deliveries.

The two parties weren't fully aligned on when certain pickups and drop-offs were supposed to happen, which contributed to the inconsistent performance.

Once the issue was addressed, the supplier's performance became much more consistent. Eventually, we felt confident enough in their ability to meet our expectations that we included them in the initiative.

Looking at the numbers alone, it would have been easy to assume that the supplier wasn't capable of meeting our shipping expectations. But by communicating with the people involved, we discovered that the problem was something that could actually be fixed.

Data can show you where to look, but understanding the people and processes behind it is often what helps you find the solution.

Learning to Communicate Beyond the Data

Something I've realized through my co-ops is that being able to analyze data is only part of what makes someone a good analyst. Just as important is being able to explain what you've found in a way that other people can understand.

At Wayfair, I worked closely with supplier relationship managers who didn't necessarily have the same familiarity with the reports and dashboards my team used every day.

When discussing performance issues, we would often walk through relevant reports to show where our information was coming from. But rather than simply pointing to a metric, we would explain the issue in simpler terms and use the data to support that explanation.

I learned that it was often more effective to start with the problem itself rather than immediately jumping into the numbers.

This was a skill I had to develop over time. I became more comfortable communicating my findings toward the end of my first co-op, but it's something I'm still actively working on.

Another important lesson was learning not to be afraid of asking questions.

When I first started, there were moments when I hesitated to ask something because I was worried it would sound like a dumb question. I was surrounded by people who understood the business, its systems, and its processes much better than I did.

But I quickly realized that the only way I was going to learn was by asking questions, even when I felt like I should already know the answer.

Understanding why processes existed, how teams worked together, or what a particular metric meant often required conversations with people who had more experience.

I've learned that asking questions isn't a sign that you don't know what you're doing. Sometimes, it's the most important step toward understanding what you're trying to solve.

Advice for Students Interested in Analytics

If you're a computer science student interested in exploring business analytics but don't have much experience with the business side, my biggest advice would be to start by figuring out what areas interest you.

Working with data in supply chain operations can look very different from working in marketing, finance, or merchandising. The technical skills might overlap, but the questions you're answering and decisions you're supporting can be completely different.

I'd recommend researching how analytics is used in industries that interest you. Look through job descriptions, explore the problems analysts work on, and pay attention to the skills that appear repeatedly.

From there, personal projects are a great way to start building experience.

You don't need access to a company's data to practice thinking like an analyst. Public datasets can help you explore trends, build visualizations, and practice drawing conclusions.

More importantly, go beyond simply creating a graph or dashboard. Think about what the data is telling you, what questions it raises, and how someone might use those findings to make a decision.

If you're looking for somewhere to start, Kaggle Datasets has publicly available datasets across many different subjects.

Finally, I'd encourage students to stay open to opportunities that might not perfectly match what they originally pictured themselves doing. Even if a role isn't exactly what you expected, there's still so much you can learn, both technically and professionally.

Looking Beyond the Numbers

Looking back at what I originally thought working in business analytics would be like, I realize just how much my perspective has changed.

I've learned that analytics is just as much about solving problems and improving processes as it is about analyzing data. Sometimes that means using performance metrics to make decisions about suppliers, while other times it means identifying inefficiencies in an internal workflow.

What I find interesting is how connected those things are. Improving an internal process might seem like a small change, but it can have a much larger impact on how a company operates and the experience it provides to the people it works with.

I'm still learning what it means to be a good analyst, but that's one of the things I've enjoyed most about these experiences. There's always another problem to investigate, another process to improve, or another perspective to consider.

The value of data isn't just in what it tells you, but in what you choose to do with it.
